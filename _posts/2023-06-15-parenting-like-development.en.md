What does it mean to pour affection into a service. Isn't it treating my code like my child? If you develop the way you raise a child, a lot of it maps over as metaphor.

If you develop the way you raise a child, you start figuring out how to help your kid run better, learn better, not get hurt. You try tests before sending them out into the world, and you study how to get better at parenting too.

If there's something a bit different from parenting in the real world, it's that in this world I can pluck my kid's heart out and graft on another one, or, if I only have the money, pluck out an eye and attach an android eye with 100x zoom. And I can even say "you don't need legs to live, so I'll take the legs off" and take the legs off. The metaphor is cruel, but that's how it is here. It suddenly makes me think I should show respect to the doctors who are actually attaching different hearts.

But why attach and detach perfectly fine limbs in the first place? Because my kid isn't actually living in the real world; they live in the computer, a bounded but infinite logical space. Usually they don't live in the whole infinite space, only in some part of it, and that part has set boundaries and conditions. To live better than anyone in this world, having legs might get in the way, or you might have to turn them into a weird monster with grotesquely oversized eyes. Seeing each part of the software and the environment it runs in as something alive also seems connected to object-orientation. Object-orientation is centered on role, responsibility, and collaboration.

Software lives and moves in the space of a computer, but it actually interacts with the real world. I sent my kid to the playground inside the apartment to play with friends, and aunties might talk to them or older kids might pick a fight. Making my code able to handle these sudden variables is a duty as a caregiver. To do that I need to know what habits my child has, how many meters they can kick a ball, how fast they pick up language, how good they are at math, and so on. Likewise I need to know how much load my software can take, which parts are fixed and hard to change, which parts are fluid, what the system is for. The best caregiver can make good decisions for the child at the right time, or ahead of time, through most of the process of growing up. The best engineer can also make good decisions about the software's future at the right time, or ahead of time. It's a similar view to engineers having to build systems that are resilient to change and stable.
