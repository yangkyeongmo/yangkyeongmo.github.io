The Edward Hopper exhibition left several lessons or thoughts, so it stuck with me. I was struck that he kept drawing studies throughout his life and explored many subjects. In pursuing painting as a way to express the inner self, and perfection in that method, he kept practicing and exploring new methods to reach the goal. I also felt a sense of kinship in that his personal temperament, like mine, was extremely introverted.

He probably didn't paint the finished work in one go. First he would have **imagined** what picture to paint. Roughly in his head, by recalling a memory or through a real subject, he would have gotten inspiration and had a rough sense of what the finished work felt like. Here, as a first step, a finished work with no physical substance was made.

Then, instead of trying to make the finished piece in one shot, he studied the rough form through **studies**. He didn't apply color; he only sketched in charcoal. He studied how to draw the appearance of stones or waves or buildings, and so on. A bit more concrete than the rough imagination. But it still isn't the finished piece. Even so, by this point you can already roughly tell what shape the finished piece will have.

The work of applying **color** and making the finished piece came after that. Once he knew the shape through studies, he applied color, a deeper layer of detail. Once you get here, a finished piece is made, with all sorts of detail added onto all the abstract concepts.

Development should be done like a painter, like an artist. A skilled painter doesn't seem to paint the finished work in one go. Shouldn't a developer be like that too. Instead of jumping straight into implementation, you should be able to **analyze the business requirements** and imagine what features are needed. Once the service-as-imagination is complete, next you can think about an **abstract structure**. Around here is also when you can try it as pseudocode. Whether you consider layers or split out separate services, you decide roughly what abstract structure you can proceed with. As this thinking takes shape, you could write a technical review document. When this stage is done, you can go into **actual implementation**. At last every abstraction waits to show its true form and have detailed color painted on. Right here you'd actually write test code, fill in the bodies of functions, apply design patterns, and use all sorts of technique.

A developer should be able to do this whole process from start to finish. Every part of the process is something to hone toward perfection with a craftsman's spirit.

I wrote this while looking at *Edward Hopper: On the Road*.
